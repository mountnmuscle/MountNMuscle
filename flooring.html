What does floor tear-out include, and why is hauling it bundled at $1.00 per square foot?

Tear-out is $1.00 per square foot and includes hauling the debris away, because in Mecklenburg County there is nowhere else for it to go. City curbside collection takes household trash, yard waste and scheduled bulky items — it does not take renovation debris. A rolled-up pile of old carpet stacked at the curb is remodeling waste, and it will sit there. Bundling the haul into the tear-out price is the only version of this that actually ends with an empty room.

What tear-out means in practice on a carpeted room: cut the carpet into manageable strips and roll them, pull the pad, and then pull the tack strip off the perimeter — that is the slow part, because tack strip is nailed into the subfloor and it splinters. Then the staples. A carpet pad in a Charlotte builder-grade house can be held down with several hundred staples per room, and every one of them has to come out or it telegraphs through a thin vinyl plank. Then the room gets swept and vacuumed down to bare subfloor.

Old laminate or floating vinyl comes up faster, since it is not fastened down, but it usually hides a foam underlayment and a layer of construction dust. Glue-down vinyl and sheet vinyl are the slow ones, and adhesive residue that has to be scraped flat is prep work, not tear-out — that is where the $70 per hour subfloor line applies. We will tell you which category you are in when we look at it, not after we have started.

If you would rather pull your own carpet to save the tear-out line, that is a legitimate way to cut the bill. Pull the carpet, the pad, the tack strip and the staples, get the debris out of the house yourself, and you are only paying the install rate. Half-finished tear-out where the staples are still in is worse than not starting, so it is all or nothing.

Why does the subfloor matter more than the plank you picked?

A floating floor copies the surface underneath it. Most LVP and laminate manufacturers want the subfloor flat to roughly 3/16 of an inch over a 10-foot span, and going outside that is the single most common cause of a failed install. Subfloor prep is $70 per hour and it is the line people most want to skip and most regret skipping.

The failure looks like this. Over a high spot, the planks bridge and you get a hollow, drummy click when you walk across it — the locking joints are being flexed with every step, and eventually one of them separates and the seam peaks. Over a low spot the floor deflects, and the same joint fatigue happens from the other direction. That is telegraphing: every hump, every dip, every raised seam in the old plywood, every ridge of dried adhesive shows up as a shadow, a squeak or a gap in the finished floor months later. The plank did not fail. The floor under it did.

Charlotte has all three common substrates and each one prepares differently. Slab on grade is common in ranch houses and additions across Steele Creek and the older south Charlotte neighborhoods — slabs are usually flat but not always level, they can have trowel ridges, control joints and cold-joint lips, and they hold moisture, so a slab install wants a moisture barrier or a plank with one attached. Crawlspace over joists with a plywood or OSB deck is the standard for most of the metro's single-family stock — here the issues are squeaks from nails backing out, soft or spongy spots from a past plumbing leak, and seams that have swelled where the panel edges meet. Second floors over joists add deflection: a bouncy span feels the same under a new floor as it did under the carpet, and no plank fixes it.

Prep work is mostly unglamorous and fast once you know what you are dealing with: screwing the deck down at the joists to kill squeaks, sanding or grinding a high seam, floating a low spot with patch compound, cutting out and replacing a section of rotted or delaminated subfloor. On a normal room it is often under an hour. When it is going to be more than that we say so before we start, with a number attached.

How do I measure my square footage, and how much flooring should I order?

Measure length times width in feet for each room, add the rooms together, and then order 7 to 10 percent more material than that number. A 400 square foot living room means buying about 430 to 440 square feet of plank. That overage is not padding — it is the material that ends up as offcuts.

Every plank that meets a wall gets cut, and the short piece is only reusable if it is long enough to start the next row without stacking a seam. Doorways, closet returns, angles and hearths all eat material. Herringbone and other patterns waste more, so lean toward 10 percent, sometimes more, on a pattern layout. It is also worth having a few spare planks left in the garage when the job is done, because dye lots change and a plank you buy two years from now will not match the floor you have.

For odd shapes, break the room into rectangles and add them up rather than trying to average a diagonal. Measure closets and count them, because they are usually forgotten and they are real square footage. Bay windows and angled walls should be measured at their widest point. If you send a rough sketch with dimensions by text to 704-237-0164 we will do the arithmetic with you and tell you what it prices out to before anyone commits.

The install minimum is 200 square feet or $500, whichever is greater, so small rooms are a real consideration. At $2.50 per square foot, 200 square feet works out to exactly $500 — anything under that still bills at the $500 minimum. If you are doing a single small bedroom, it is usually worth pairing it with a hallway or a second room on the same visit.

What actually happens on install day — acclimation, transitions and trim?

Get the material into the house at least 48 hours before install, and check your specific manufacturer's sheet, since some ask for more. Boxes should sit flat in the room where the floor is going, not stacked in a hot garage or a cold truck bed, so the planks reach the temperature and humidity they will live at.

Skipping acclimation is what causes the classic call three weeks later. Rigid-core LVP moves less than laminate, but it still moves — a plank installed cold and then warmed by afternoon sun through a south-facing window expands, and if it has nowhere to go it pushes the row up in the middle of the floor or peaks a seam at a doorway. That is also why the floor is installed with an expansion gap around the entire perimeter, typically about a quarter inch, and why the floor should never be pinched tight against a wall, a door casing or a kitchen island toe kick.

That perimeter gap gets covered, not filled. Quarter round or shoe molding over the existing baseboard is the usual answer and is the cheaper path, because it does not require pulling and reinstalling the base. Door casings get undercut so the plank slides underneath and the cut disappears. Where the new floor meets a different surface — tile at a bathroom, carpet on the stairs, a slab that steps down at a garage door — it takes a transition: a T-molding for two floors of the same height, a reducer where the new floor is taller, an end cap at a sliding door. Buy transitions and quarter round in the same product line as your floor when you can, since color-matched trim from the same manufacturer looks far better than a generic strip.

One more thing worth planning for: doors. A new floor over a slab or a thick underlayment can raise the height enough that an interior door drags. Trimming a door bottom is straightforward, but it is easier to know in advance than to discover it at 6pm.

Do you install tile, and what flooring work should you hire someone else for?

Yes — floor tile and backsplash tile are on the rate card, billed at the same $2.50 per square foot install rate as LVP, with the same 200 square foot or $500 minimum. Standard thinset-and-grout tile work over a properly prepped substrate is what we quote every week, and it goes through the same on-site measure and confirmed-price process as every other flooring job.

What is a different job entirely is a full wet-area rebuild — waterproofing a shower pan from scratch, a curbless entry, a steam shower, or anything where the waterproofing membrane itself is the thing being built. That carries its own liability and its own trade, and we will tell you straight which side of that line your job falls on before anyone starts cutting tile.

Beyond that, our rate card covers LVP, laminate, tile, herringbone and premium pattern installation, tear-out and haul away, and subfloor prep. Hardwood refinishing is a specialist trade with dust-containment equipment, drum and edge sanders and a finish schedule, and a bad sanding job cannot be undone. Carpet installation needs a power stretcher and a kicker and someone who does it every week. Structural work — sagging joists, sistering, pier and beam repair in a crawlspace, anything holding the house up — belongs to a structural contractor. Standing water, an active plumbing leak, or a subfloor that is wet rather than just stained needs the source fixed by a plumber and the area dried before any floor goes down over it. Suspected asbestos in 9x9 tile or old sheet vinyl backing, and lead paint in a pre-1978 house, both need proper testing and abatement.

One practical note on scope: residential flooring jobs of this size sit well under the $40,000 threshold at which North Carolina requires a general contractor license.
